Career Guidance

Build a practical career direction

You do not need to have your entire career planned. Use these lessons to understand your options and choose a useful next step based on your current situation.

4 lessons
1 Explore interests and strengths Identify work that may fit how you think, contribute, and learn.

Begin with evidence from your life

Career interests are not limited to favorite school subjects or hobbies. Think about tasks that hold your attention, problems you enjoy solving, and situations where other people rely on you.

Interests

Notice the subjects, activities, industries, or problems that you willingly spend time exploring.

Strengths

Consider skills you have practiced, personal qualities you use consistently, and tasks that others trust you to handle.

Work preferences

Think about whether you prefer teamwork or independent work, predictable routines or variety, and hands-on or computer-based tasks.

Try this reflection

  1. Write down three activities that make you feel focused or useful.
  2. Identify the skill you use during each activity.
  3. Search for two occupations that regularly use one or more of those skills.

You do not need to be an expert before exploring a field. Interests can grow as you gain experience.

2 Set realistic goals and find suitable jobs Turn a broad career idea into achievable next steps.

Choose a direction you can act on

A useful career goal connects something you want with a step you can take. It should reflect your current experience, responsibilities, available time, and opportunities to learn.

From broad idea to practical goal

Instead of “I want a good healthcare career,” try: “During the next month, I will compare three entry-level healthcare roles and identify the training each one requires.”

Evaluate job opportunities

Read the complete posting and compare it with your current needs. Look beyond the job title.

  • Confirm the main duties and required qualifications.
  • Consider location, schedule, pay, transportation, and physical requirements.
  • Separate required qualifications from skills an employer describes as preferred.
  • Research unfamiliar employers and confirm that the opportunity is legitimate.
  • Apply when you can truthfully demonstrate most of the essential requirements.

Create a three-step goal

  1. Choose one role or career area to investigate.
  2. Identify one skill, credential, or experience commonly requested for that work.
  3. Choose one realistic action and a date by which you will complete it.
3 Organize your search and prepare for work Build routines that make job searching and starting work more manageable.

Use a repeatable job-search routine

A job search is easier to manage when applications, follow-ups, and preparation are recorded in one place. A smaller number of thoughtful applications is often more useful than many rushed applications.

  • Set aside specific times each week to search and apply.
  • Save promising jobs before their closing dates.
  • Record every submitted application in your Application Tracker.
  • Keep copies of the job descriptions you applied to.
  • Add interview dates and appropriate follow-up reminders.

Build workplace readiness

Workplace readiness includes the habits that help colleagues and customers depend on you. These habits matter in every industry.

Reliability

Arrive on time, communicate early about problems, and complete agreed responsibilities.

Communication

Listen carefully, ask useful questions, and use a respectful tone in person and online.

Learning

Take notes, accept constructive feedback, and ask for clarification before guessing.

Prepare for your first week

Write down how you would confirm your schedule, plan transportation, learn the dress expectations, and contact your supervisor if an emergency occurs.

4 Navigate a first job or career change Recognize transferable experience and choose an appropriate entry point.

Guidance for first-time job seekers

Paid employment is not the only source of useful experience. School projects, caregiving, clubs, athletics, volunteering, and community activities can demonstrate real skills when described honestly.

  • Identify responsibilities you completed consistently.
  • Describe what you did, who it helped, and what you learned.
  • Consider jobs that provide clear training and realistic entry-level expectations.
  • Ask trusted adults, instructors, or community contacts whether they can serve as references.

Guidance for career changers

A career change does not erase your previous experience. Focus on skills that transfer to the new field while being clear about what you are still learning.

  • Compare your previous responsibilities with the duties in the new occupation.
  • Identify transferable skills such as leadership, customer service, analysis, organization, safety, or project coordination.
  • Research whether a short course, certification, volunteer role, or project could close an important skill gap.
  • Expect that your first role in a new field may be a bridge to your longer-term goal.

Create a transferable-skills statement

Complete this sentence truthfully: “My experience with ______ prepared me to ______, which would help me contribute as a ______.”

Never invent experience to appear qualified. A clear explanation of genuine transferable skills is more credible and more useful.

Interview Preparation

Prepare to speak with confidence

Interviews are conversations about how your genuine experience, interests, and working style relate to an opportunity. Preparation helps you communicate clearly without memorizing or inventing answers.

4 lessons
1 Prepare for common interview questions Research the role and organize the points you want an employer to understand.

Prepare before you practice answers

Begin by reviewing the job description, employer, location, schedule, and interview instructions. Identify the skills and responsibilities the employer mentions most often.

  • Review the application and résumé you submitted.
  • Research what the employer provides and who it serves.
  • Choose genuine examples that demonstrate relevant skills.
  • Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for the interviewer.
  • Confirm the interview time, time zone, format, and contact information.

Common questions and what they explore

Tell me about yourself

Give a brief professional introduction connecting your current situation, relevant experience, and interest in the role.

Why are you interested?

Explain what genuinely interests you about the work and how it relates to your goals or skills.

What are your strengths?

Choose a real strength, provide evidence, and explain how it would help you perform the job.

What is a weakness?

Select an honest area for improvement and describe the practical steps you are taking to improve it.

Why should we hire you?

Summarize the relevant value you can offer without criticizing other applicants or exaggerating.

Do you have questions?

Ask about expectations, training, team structure, performance, or the next stage of the process.

Create a 60-second introduction

  1. Describe your current professional situation.
  2. Name one or two relevant strengths.
  3. Explain why this opportunity interests you.

Practice the key points instead of memorizing every word. A natural answer usually sounds more credible.

2 Answer behavioral questions with structure Use a truthful example to explain what happened, what you did, and what you learned.

Recognize behavioral questions

Behavioral questions often begin with phrases such as “Tell me about a time,” “Describe a situation,” or “Give me an example.” Employers use them to understand how you have responded to real situations.

Common behavioral topics

  • Solving a problem
  • Working with a difficult person
  • Learning from a mistake
  • Managing competing priorities
  • Helping a customer or teammate
  • Taking initiative

Use the STAR structure

Situation

Briefly explain the setting and enough background for the interviewer to understand the challenge.

Task

Describe your responsibility or the outcome you needed to achieve.

Action

Explain the specific steps you took. Focus on your contribution while acknowledging teamwork.

Result

Describe the outcome, what changed, or what you learned. Use accurate details rather than invented numbers.

Build an evidence bank

Write down four genuine experiences from employment, school, volunteering, projects, athletics, caregiving, or community activities. For each one, record the situation, your responsibility, your actions, and the result.

Never create a fictional example because it sounds more impressive. If you have not faced the exact situation, say so and explain how you would approach it using related genuine experience.

3 Practice for different interview formats Prepare for telephone, virtual, and in-person conversations.

Practice actively

Reading advice is useful, but speaking answers aloud helps you notice unclear explanations, rushed pacing, and missing details.

  • Practice with a trusted person who can ask follow-up questions.
  • Record yourself answering one or two questions, then listen for clarity and unnecessary repetition.
  • Practice pausing briefly before answering.
  • Prepare short and detailed versions of important examples.

Telephone interviews

Find a quiet location, charge your phone, confirm reception, keep water nearby, and speak clearly. Since visual cues are unavailable, avoid speaking over the interviewer.

Virtual interviews

Test the meeting link, camera, microphone, internet connection, lighting, and display name. Close distracting applications and look toward the camera when speaking.

In-person interviews

Plan your route, transportation, parking, and arrival time. Bring requested materials and treat everyone you meet respectfully.

Run a five-question practice interview

  1. Tell me about yourself.
  2. Why does this role interest you?
  3. Describe a problem you solved.
  4. What would you like to improve?
  5. What questions do you have for us?

Afterward, identify one answer to clarify and one strength to preserve.

4 Use an interview-day and follow-up plan Reduce avoidable stress and respond professionally after the conversation.

Interview-day checklist

  • Recheck the time, location, format, and interviewer’s name.
  • Review the job’s main responsibilities and your most relevant examples.
  • Choose clean, appropriate clothing suited to the employer and role.
  • Bring or prepare any requested documents.
  • Silence unnecessary notifications and remove distractions.
  • Arrive or connect early without joining excessively early.
  • Listen fully, ask for clarification when necessary, and answer truthfully.

Follow up professionally

When appropriate, send a brief thank-you message within one business day. Mention the role, express appreciation, and refer to a useful point from the conversation.

Follow-up structure

  1. Thank the interviewer for their time.
  2. Restate your genuine interest in the role.
  3. Mention one relevant detail you appreciated learning.
  4. Close professionally with your name.

If the employer provided a decision timeline, wait until that date has passed before requesting an update. If no timeline was provided, a polite inquiry after several business days may be appropriate.

Record your next steps

Update your PathForge Application Tracker with the interview information, personal notes, and a reasonable follow-up date while the conversation is still fresh.

A rejection does not automatically mean you performed poorly. Employers consider many factors, including experience, timing, internal needs, and the applicant pool.

Résumé Guidance

Present your experience clearly

A résumé is a focused summary of your genuine qualifications. These lessons explain what to include, how to describe your experience, and how to make thoughtful adjustments for different opportunities.

4 lessons
1 Learn résumé fundamentals Understand contact information, summaries, objectives, and basic organization.

What a résumé should accomplish

A résumé helps an employer quickly understand your relevant skills, experience, education, and accomplishments. It should be readable, truthful, and focused on the opportunity.

Contact information

Include your name, a reliable telephone number, a professional email address, and your general location. A full street address is usually unnecessary.

Professional summary

Briefly describe relevant experience, strengths, and the value you can offer. Use evidence instead of unsupported claims.

Career objective

An objective can help first-time job seekers or career changers explain the type of opportunity they are pursuing and the skills they bring.

Common section order

  1. Contact information
  2. Summary or objective, when useful
  3. Skills
  4. Experience
  5. Education
  6. Projects, volunteer work, or certifications

The order can change. Place the most relevant and convincing information where an employer can find it easily.

Plan your sections

List the sections that would best represent your current qualifications. Then identify which section contains your strongest evidence for the type of job you want.

Do not include passwords, identification numbers, banking information, or other sensitive personal data on a résumé.

2 Describe skills, experience, and accomplishments Show what you did, how you contributed, and what resulted from your work.

Choose meaningful skills

Include skills you can genuinely demonstrate. These may include technical abilities, tools, languages, certifications, communication, organization, customer service, leadership, or safety practices.

  • Use specific skill names instead of broad phrases such as “good with computers.”
  • Prioritize skills relevant to the opportunity.
  • Be prepared to explain where and how you used each listed skill.
  • Do not list software, languages, or credentials you cannot actually use or do not possess.

Write experience with evidence

Experience entries typically include the organization, role, location, dates, and concise accomplishment statements. Experience can come from paid employment, internships, self-employment, or other genuine work.

Use action, context, and result

Weak: “Responsible for helping customers.”

Stronger: “Helped customers locate products and resolve basic questions during busy evening shifts.”

Accomplishment-writing principles

  • Begin with a clear action verb.
  • Explain the task or problem you addressed.
  • Describe who benefited or what improved.
  • Use numbers only when they are accurate and meaningful.
  • Give enough context for someone outside the organization to understand.

Strengthen one responsibility

Choose one genuine responsibility. Rewrite it by answering: What did I do? How did I do it? Who or what benefited? What was the accurate result?

Never invent metrics or enlarge your responsibilities. A modest, specific, truthful accomplishment is more credible than an impressive claim you cannot support.

3 Present education, projects, and volunteer work Use relevant experience from inside and outside traditional employment.

Education

Include the school or training provider, program, credential, and completion date or expected completion date when relevant. You may also include relevant coursework, honors, or activities when they support your application.

  • Do not claim a degree or certification before it has been earned.
  • Clearly label education that is currently in progress.
  • Usually omit elementary or middle-school education.
  • Include grades only when useful, accurate, and appropriate.

Projects

Projects can demonstrate applied skills when you have limited paid experience or when you are entering a new field. Include the project purpose, your contribution, tools used, and outcome.

Volunteer and community experience

Volunteer work can show reliability, service, teamwork, planning, leadership, and other genuine skills. Describe it with the same care you would use for paid employment.

School project

Explain the assignment, your role, the process, and the finished result.

Personal project

Describe what you created or investigated and the skills you practiced.

Volunteer work

Identify the organization, your responsibilities, and how your work supported others.

Build an experience inventory

List employment, education, projects, clubs, caregiving, volunteering, and community activities. Beside each one, record the skills and responsibilities you can truthfully demonstrate.

4 Avoid common mistakes and tailor thoughtfully Improve relevance and readability without changing the truth.

Common résumé mistakes

  • Spelling, grammar, or inconsistent formatting
  • Incorrect telephone numbers or email addresses
  • Dense paragraphs that are difficult to scan
  • Unexplained abbreviations or internal job titles
  • Irrelevant information that hides stronger evidence
  • Vague claims without examples
  • Inaccurate dates, skills, credentials, or results
  • Decorative elements that make the document difficult to read

What tailoring means

Tailoring means emphasizing the genuine information most relevant to a particular opportunity. It does not mean changing facts to match every job description.

  1. Identify the posting’s main responsibilities and required qualifications.
  2. Compare those requirements with your genuine skills and experience.
  3. Move the most relevant evidence into prominent positions.
  4. Use accurate terminology that both you and the employer understand.
  5. Remove unrelated details when they distract from stronger qualifications.

Truthful tailoring example

If a posting emphasizes customer communication, you might place genuine customer-service experience before less relevant responsibilities. You should not add customer-service experience you never had.

Compare without rewriting

Choose one job posting and underline its three most important requirements. Then identify where your existing information provides truthful evidence for each requirement.
